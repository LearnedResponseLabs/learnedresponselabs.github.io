Explainers · Galashin’s Conjecture 4.3

The idea, in seven steps

How the argument works, from the question to what it gives: angles become a point, the point turns out to be the slope of a convex bowl, and minimizing reads the angles back. Each step has one picture and leads to the cell explorer, the reading edition and the paper for the details.

1234567
1

Angles make a point

A cell is named by a permutation, here the window 3, 5, 1, 2, 4 on five labels. Draw each label’s strand across a circle; two strands that cross give an edge of the crossing graph, seven edges here. Put an angle θp on each label. The angles are admissible when every crossing edge e = p → q has its gap δe strictly between 0 and π.

Galashin builds, from admissible angles, a point of the Grassmannian: the critical measurement Measf(θ). The points it reaches form the critical cell. Turning every angle of one connected piece of the crossing graph by the same amount changes nothing, so one angle per piece is fixed: that is the gauge slice. His Conjecture 4.3 says that on the gauge slice the measurement is a homeomorphism: different angles give different points, every point of the cell is reached, and the angles depend continuously on the point.

2

The plan: the slope of a bowl

In one variable, a function whose slope keeps increasing takes each slope exactly once; to find where the slope equals a given number, tilt the graph by that number and look for the bottom. The same holds in any dimension: the gradient of a strictly convex function on a convex region is one-to-one, and its inverse is found by minimizing.

So the strategy is to show that the measurement is, in the right coordinates, the gradient of a strictly convex function of the angles. Then one-to-one is automatic, and reading the angles back is a minimization that a computer can carry out.

3

New coordinates

The point Galashin measures has necklace minors: n of its Plücker coordinates, one for each row of the cell’s Grassmann necklace, and none of them vanishes on the cell. After the Muller–Speyer twist, Galashin’s formula writes each twisted necklace minor, up to sign, as a product of factors 2 sin δe, one for each crossing edge, raised to integer exponents.

Taking logarithms turns those products into sums: each coordinate becomes a linear combination of the numbers log sin δe, with integer coefficients that come from the necklace, plus a constant. The twist is needed only to prove this; to recover a point it suffices to read ratios of the ordinary necklace minors (Remark 13, Corollary 32).

4

The measurement is a gradient

Now subtract each coordinate from the next one around the necklace, and relabel. The integer exponents collapse to something very simple: for each crossing edge, −1 at its tail and +1 at its head. This is the incidence matrix B of the crossing graph, and the identity PfDMN = B is Proposition 10.

With it, the measurement read in these coordinates is exactly the gradient of the potential Ψf(θ) = Σe L(δe), where L′(u) = log sin u. One more derivative gives the Hessian B diag(cot δe) BT: a graph Laplacian whose conductances are the cotangents of the gaps.

5

Winding cycles tame the obtuse angles

There is a catch. When a gap δe is obtuse its cotangent is negative, so the potential is not convex edge by edge. The way out is to group the edges. The crossing edges of every cell split into cycles that each go around the circle exactly once (Proposition 15), and around such a cycle the gaps add up to exactly π.

For gaps that add up to π, a cotangent inequality shows that the cycle’s contribution to the Hessian is never negative, and is zero only when the cycle’s angles all move together. Adding the cycles up, the Hessian is positive semidefinite, and the only directions where it vanishes are the component shifts that the gauge slice already removed (Proposition 26).

6

Putting it together

On the gauge slice, which is convex, the potential is strictly convex. So its gradient is one-to-one, and since the Hessian is nonsingular the gradient is locally invertible with a continuous inverse. Galashin’s gauge invariance shows that the slice already reaches the whole critical cell. Together these make the measurement a homeomorphism from the gauge slice onto the critical cell: Theorem 1, a proof candidate for Conjecture 4.3 for every loopless cell.

The inverse is constructive. To read the angles back from a point, compute its coordinates y, then minimize the tilted bowl Ψf − ⟨y, ·⟩ over the gauge slice; the unique minimizer is the answer (Corollary 31).

7

What it gives

The ratios of the n ordinary necklace minors determine a critical point (Corollary 32), and the critical cell is a real-analytic submanifold on which the measurement is a real-analytic diffeomorphism (Corollary 33). When every gap is at least some η > 0, the Hessian sits between two multiples of the crossing graph’s Laplacian, with a sharp constant for a single cycle (Theorem 28, Corollary 29), which makes the inverse Lipschitz in these coordinates.

The potential has a geometric reading: up to a constant it is minus a sum of Lobachevsky functions, a hyperbolic volume. And through Galashin’s shift, the theorem gives a homeomorphism between critical cells of neighbouring ranks, which matches critical electrical networks with critical Ising models of the same region (Corollary 2).

Below the top cell: the bow-tie

Galashin proved his conjecture for the top cells (his Theorem 4.4). In a top cell every pair of labels crosses: in the top cell of Gr(3,5) all ten pairs are edges of the crossing graph. Below the top some pairs no longer cross, and new things happen. The videos use the bow-tie to show them: five labels, window 3, 1, 5, 2, 4, in the same Grassmannian, whose six crossing edges form two triangles that share label 2.

Pairs that do not cross. Four pairs of the bow-tie are not crossings: {1, 5} and {3, 4} are aligned, and {1, 4} and {3, 5} are misaligned, in Galashin’s two directions. Misaligned pairs can enter his formula for the necklace minors; on the bow-tie their exponents vanish on every row, so only the six crossing edges reach the gradient.

Collisions. Nothing ties the angle of label 3 to the angle of label 5, so their points can meet on the circle while every gap stays strictly between 0 and π. On a top cell that cannot happen: every pair crosses, and two crossing labels at one point would have a gap of 0 or π. The theorem covers these collisions: Galashin’s formulas exclude coinciding points only for crossing pairs, so a collision of noncrossing labels is an ordinary interior point of the gauge slice (the proof of Corollary 33).

The bowl splits. Each triangle is a winding-one cycle, and every crossing edge lies in exactly one of them. In the coordinates of the gaps the gauge slice is a product of two triangles, one per cycle, and the potential is a sum of two bowls; reading the angles back solves the two triangles separately. That happens because the cycles share only a corner. In general they do not come apart, but the potential is still one convex bowl, so its bottom is still the only answer.

What is checked, and how

The combinatorial and convexity steps, 3 to 6, are formalized in Lean 4: their statements are frozen apart from their proofs, and an independent tool checks that each proof proves exactly its statement. The analytic inputs that come from Galashin’s paper (his Propositions 4.2, 5.1 and 5.12 and his Lemma 5.13) and the Muller–Speyer twist enter the formal theorem as stated hypotheses. The result is a proof candidate: it has been reviewed internally, and external review is pending. The reading edition sets every formal statement beside the paper.
